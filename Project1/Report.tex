\section{Introduction}

The sport of mixed-martial arts (MMA) is widely regarded as one of the most unpredicatable- and dynamic sports one can spectacte. A sudden knockout, freak injury or a mistake giving way to a submission, 
it's a sport where anything, at any moment, can happen. When each bout is equally probable to be a controlled, one-sided domination as it has being an explosive war of violence and unpredicatability,
the question becomes: can an outcome of such an event even be predicted? Let alone consistently? \par

The UFC (Ultimate Fighting Championship) is currently the largest professional MMA organization with an extensive record of fights spanning across its 30+ years of existence. With such a 
long and lustrious history, finding a suitable dataset was not a a difficult task and \href{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/anthonysz/ufc-fight-data-2026/data}{Kaggle, UFC Fight Data - 2026} 
provided just that.\par

With records dating back to March 2010 all the way to May of this year, it covers over 7000 fights and includes 57 features that describe each fighters stats., physical attributes and betting odds, and more.
The set also includes a binary classifier on the winning figher (whether the figher in the red corner won or lost), which works as a great predictor feature.\par

Given this predictor feature we want to take note of the given baseline: the red figher has a win rate of 58\% against blue's 42\%, which is a significant gap.\par

With this benchmark, the main goal of this project then becomes two-fold:  
\begin{itemize}
    \item Develop a model that can outperform the simple majority class baselines and accurately predict a UFC fight based purely on statistcal differences between the fighters.
    \item See which attributes/features contribute the most to a outcome and by how much of a margin.
\end{itemize}

\section{Process}
After having picked a candidate dataset, we needed to verify its quality and usability for a potential model. For this part of the process we conducted some simple, but effective EDA that would answer 
those questions.\par 

\subsection{Brief EDA}

We first checked for any obivious outliers and missing data values by skimming over its metadata on Kaggle, of which there were few and far between. For the next step, we moved to analysing the datasets features 
and it came to us as a pleasant suprise that the dataset didn't just include each fighters raw statistics, but rather the difference between those values at the point before a given bout took place, 
e.g., sig\_str\_diff (the difference between the red fighters and blue fighters total significant strikes). With the goal of the model being to predict a winner based on comparisons between such attributes, 
having them pre-calculated lightened the work load significantly.\par   

Before commiting to this particular dataset, we decided to look into other projects/research material that was conducted using the same dataset. One in particular struck as particularly interesting, that is:
\href{https://www.kaggle.com/code/tahaberkterekli/can-we-predict-ufc-fight-outcomes}{Can We Predict UFC Fight Outcomes?}. Not only did the results of this article all but confirm to us our initial questions 
regarding the value of the dataset, it also gave us a good way to validate our own work against theirs. More importantly however, is that it gave us a clear ceiling for the accuracy that any model can achieve
and why. There are two cases that need to be examined:

\textbf{Case 1:} A model that is only trained and tested on a dataset that includes a fighters individual statistics (age, significant strikes difference, etc.) is capped around the low-mid 60s for accurately 
predicting an outcome; a remarkably high number for such an unpredicatable sport.

\textbf{Case 2:} Including odds (red\_odds, blue\_odds, odds\_diff) as a feature of the model significantly improves its accuracy (~70\%), however since it's a feature that exists only as as product of external 
assessment on a fighters favorability in a given matchup, it collides with the stated purpose of the project. 

This becomes more relevant later on, but for now its good to have in mind as we proceed.

\subsection{Data Cleaning}

After having conducted some initial pruning into the dataset and verified its usability we proceeded to a more extensive data- cleaning and analysis for feature selection.

\subsubsection{Outliers \& Missing Data}

\paragraph*{The Return Columns}
The values of the \verb|red\_return| and \verb|blue\_return| columns reflect the payout a \$1 bet would return. Including them would cause target leakage since they tell the 
model exactly who won (loser's return being 0) and in turn lead to a 100\% model prediction rate accuracy; something we obiviously wanted to avoid.

\paragraph*{Stance Outliers}
We noticed in the \verb|red\_stance| and \verb|blue\_stance| columns that there were a few rows with faulty classifications and missing data ("Switch " with a trailing space and "Unknown"),
that needed to be excluded. We also found one other classification called "Open Stance", which describes an opposite stance matchup, which was only found in the \verb|blue\_stance column|. 
Since we already have the three main stances ("Orthodox", "Southpaw" and "Switch") it becomes a redundant categorical value and only creates conflict.

\paragraph*{Weight Class Outliers}
In the column \verb|weight\_class| there we found the value "Catch Weight" which describes fights where one or both fighters missed their target weight and therefore fought outside 
one of the 12 regulartory weight classes. This is by definition an outlier to the standard procedure of an MMA bout and should therefore be excluded.

\paragraph*{Outliers in Difference Columns}
In the \verb|rounds\_diff| column we found one extreme outlier where the value was over 440. We excluded this due to it being an impossible data value when
looking at either fighters records (i.e. total fights).\\
In the \verb|reach\_diff| column there is a single outlier where the reach difference was over 186 caused by one of the fighters reach being measured 0 cm.
This is an obivious error caused by a missing data value so we naturally excluded it.

We initially included fighters age as a classifier, whose values were pooled into buckets of age ranges (e.g., 18 - 23) but a realization quickly arose. That is, as we increased the 
number of buckets (age ranges) the accuracy of the model increased in parallel, which suggest that there is too much variability in a fighters standing in even as little as a year. 
And since the dataset already included the age difference between fighters for each bout, which is the effect we're interested in, we decided to drop it entirely. \par

\subsubsection{Feature Selection}
Along with the aforementioned classifiers, we settled on inlcuding only the \verb|\_diff| features due to them providing the model the exact metrics needed to evaluate a fighters standing 
when making a prediction. The one exception to this was the \verb|odds_diff| column, and like stated before, the issue with that feature is that it signals to the model which fighter 
was favored going into a given matchup; biasing it to one outcome based only on external assessment rather than its own.\par

But although that was the case, it still intrigued us to see what a model with that feature included would deliver. We therefore developed two sets of selected features, one with and one without
the \verb|odds\_diff| feature; so we can see first hand how much it impacts the models prediction.    

\subsubsection{Feature Engineering}
The impact of the age difference between two fighters varies significantly depending on where said difference occurs on the age-axis. In a bout between a 25 year old and a 28 year old, the age difference
is not nearly as significant as one between a 32 year old and a 35 year old; and the data shows this. In title fights, we see that fighters over 35 have a collective win-loss record of 50-75 across all weight classes:\par

\begin{figure}[h]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{media/title_winrate_by_age.png}

    \label{fig:ufc_winrate1}
\end{figure}

The distribution is however very skewed, with the heavier weight classes (Middleweight, Light Heavyweight and Heavyweight) boasting a much better higher win-to-loss ratio than the lighter classes; showing that the 
ligher classes are a lot less forgiving:   

\begin{figure}[h]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{media/title_winrate_by_age_division.png}
    \caption{ UFC title fight win rate by age \& division }

Instead of including just the raw age difference, which doesn't take this disparity into account, we created a new feature called \verb|age_diff_x_avg| that numerically encodes how much \verb|age_diff's|
effect should be amplified or dampened based on how old the pair is on average.

\subsubsection{Data Split}
When it came to splitting the data into dedicated training- and test sets, two questions arose: on which variable does the splitting occur and by how much. We ultimately landed on \verb|fight_date|
as our splitting variable due to it providing us two complete datasets that include every selected feature, and selecting June 1st. 2023 as our splitting point gave us clean 80/20 split.
